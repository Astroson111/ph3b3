# Charles and Eliza
### A Short Story
*Inspired by true events*

---

## Part One — The Scout

The Army gave Charles a motorcycle and told him to ride ahead.

He didn't mind. He'd grown up on a farm in Kentucky where the work was always waiting and nobody thanked you for doing it. Riding ahead of a column of tanks through the hedgerow country of Normandy wasn't so different. You went out. You looked. You came back. Or you didn't.

He had good instincts. He knew when a road felt wrong before he could say why. He'd brake, let the engine idle, listen. Sometimes it was nothing. Sometimes it was the thing that would have killed him.

On the morning it happened, the road felt wrong.

He stopped the motorcycle. Killed the engine. The silence came in around him — birds, wind, the distant sound of artillery like someone moving furniture in another room. The tank column was a quarter mile back. He was alone the way he was always alone out here, which was completely.

He looked at the road. Then he looked at the field beside it.

Then he looked at the tank that had gotten off the road and into that field, now sitting still and dark at a bad angle, one track buried, the crew inside not moving.

He already knew what the field was before he reached it.

He left the motorcycle on the road. He found a length of antenna wire from his kit — stiff, about eighteen inches. He got down on his hands and knees at the edge of the field.

He started tapping.

One inch at a time. Forward. Then to the side. Patient the way you had to be patient when a thing would kill you if you rushed it. He wasn't afraid exactly. He was just working the problem the way you worked any problem — find the edge of it, then find the next edge, then the next.

He reached the tank. He knocked on the hull. Three men came out.

He tapped them back out the same way he'd come in.

He never found out what hit him. He was almost to the road when the shrapnel caught him across the left side and put him down. Not a mine. Something else — a round, maybe, from somewhere he hadn't looked. He lay in the dirt for a moment looking at the sky, which was the same color as the inside of a helmet.

He got up.

He walked back to the motorcycle. He couldn't ride it. He walked beside it instead, pushing it with one hand, the other hand pressed to his side, until he reached the column.

Someone took the motorcycle. Someone else got him into a jeep.

He didn't say much. There wasn't much to say.

## Part Two — The Nurse

Eliza had come from County Clare on a ship that took eleven days and made her sick for nine of them.

She had not come because she was told to. She had come because she had read about what was happening in Europe and decided that her hands were capable of more than they were currently being asked to do. She was twenty-two years old and already tired of being underestimated.

The aid station was a tent that smelled like antiseptic and copper. She had been working for sixteen hours when they brought the scout in.

He sat down on the cot without being told to. He didn't make a sound while she worked. He watched her hands with an expression she recognized — not trust exactly, more like assessment. He was deciding whether she knew what she was doing.

She did.

"You walked here," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Pushed a motorcycle," he said.

She looked at him. He looked back. There was something direct about him that she respected immediately — no performance, no bravado. Just a man who had been hurt and was now in the process of deciding what to do about it.

"This is going to require stitches," she said.

"Alright," he said.

"It's going to hurt."

"Alright," he said again, in exactly the same tone.

She worked. He didn't flinch. Outside the tent the war continued its argument with itself.

When she was done she told him he needed to rest. He nodded once like he was filing the information away for later. She had the feeling he was going to ignore her.

"I mean it," she said.

"I know," he said.

"Do you?"

He looked at her again with that same assessing look. Then something shifted in it — not softness exactly. More like recognition.

"Yes," he said. "I do."

---

## Part Three — After

He came back to the aid station two days later. He wasn't hurt. He stood at the entrance of the tent with his helmet in his hands and asked if she was there.

She was.

He didn't say much. He wasn't a man who said much. But he came back, and he kept coming back, and eventually the war moved and they moved with it — separately, always separately, because that was what the war required — but they found each other again in Belgium, and again somewhere in Germany, and by the time it was over the finding had become something neither of them questioned anymore.

She never went back to County Clare. Not to live.

He never went back to the farm in Kentucky. Not to stay.

They built something instead. Something that didn't have a name for a while and then had several. A life. A family. A long unspoken agreement to keep going no matter what came next.

There was always something that came next. He went where they sent him. She went where she could. They met in the middle of whatever the world was doing and they stayed quiet about most of it.

When people asked him about the war — any of the wars — he would say the same thing.

*Just what they needed at the right time.*

She would hear him say it from across the room and she would know what it cost him to make it sound like nothing.

She never told him that. Some things you carry for a person without telling them you're carrying it.

That was its own kind of love. Maybe the deepest kind.

---

*They are buried together.*
*That's the whole story.*
